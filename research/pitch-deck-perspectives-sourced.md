## 1. Sequoia Capital — the narrative arc (V1, V4)

**Source:** https://articles.sequoiacap.com/writing-a-business-plan ; presentation mechanics: https://articles.sequoiacap.com/how-to-present-to-investors (Aaref Hilaly).

**Doctrine.** A ten-part narrative order, not a fixed slide count: company purpose (one declarative sentence) → problem → solution → why now → market potential → competition/alternatives → business model → team → financials → vision. "How to Present to Investors" adds: open with *What's changed? / What you do / Fast facts*, finish the presentation in ~20 minutes, and leave time for discussion.

**What V1/V4 do because of it.** Takeaway-claim slide titles in the Sequoia order; one-sentence purpose on slide 2; explicit why-now; vision-weighted close. V4 is the vision-heavy reading of the same arc (flagged honestly: V1 and V4 share a source family — their distinctness is angle and imagery, not framework).

## 2. Guy Kawasaki — 10/20/30 (V2)

**Source:** https://guykawasaki.com/the-102030-rule/ (canonical restatement; the original Garage Technology Ventures post is no longer reliably online).

**Doctrine.** Exactly 10 slides, 20 minutes, no font under 30 points. The rule forces compression: problem → value proposition → market → competition → model → team → projections → status → ask.

**What V2 does because of it.** Exactly 10 slides; oversized numerals as the design system (the 30-point rule taken to its typographic extreme); no slide that cannot be read across a loud room.

## 3. Y Combinator / Kevin Hale — legible, simple, obvious (V3)

**Sources:** https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/how-to-design-a-better-pitch-deck/ ; https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6q-how-to-pitch-your-startup ; https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/guide-to-demo-day-pitches .

**Doctrine.** Identify the 5–7 ideas investors must remember; make every slide legible (large bold type, contrast), simple (one idea per slide), obvious (understandable at a glance, nothing subtle). Story first, slides support it.

**What V3 does because of it.** Traction appears by slide 3 (evidence-forward), monospace annotations and schematic linework keep each slide to one claim, and no slide carries a second idea.

## 4. Nancy Duarte — the sparkline (V5)

**Sources:** https://www.duarte.com/resources/books/resonate/ ; TED: https://www.ted.com/talks/nancy_duarte_the_secret_structure_of_great_talks .

**Doctrine.** Great presentations alternate between "what is" (the audience's reality) and "what could be" (the better future), creating tension that resolves in a call to action.

**What V5 does because of it.** The one-Saturday timeline is the sparkline in miniature: each timestamped scene pairs the venue's reality with the measured "could be," resolving in the ask. (Anchor noted as style-match: the brief predates this doc.)

## 5. DocSend × Tom Eisenmann (HBS) — the data-driven deck (V6)

**Sources:** https://www.docsend.com/blog/a-brief-anatomy-of-a-successful-seed-raise/ ; https://www.docsend.com/blog/data-driven-answers-to-frequently-asked-questions-on-startup-fundraising/ ; DocSend/HBS study summary: https://innovation.ucsd.edu/startup/startup-toolkit/Pitch-Deck-Template.pdf ; pre-meeting deck guide: https://www.docsend.com/blog/how-to-build-a-pre-meeting-pitch-deck/ .

**Doctrine (quantified).** Study of 200 successful Seed/Series A raises ($360M+ raised): successful decks average ~19 slides (keep ≤20); investors average 3 min 44 s per deck and only ~58% of decks are viewed to completion — the opening must carry the thesis; the team slide appears in 100% of successful decks, placed first or last (never middle); financials are the most-viewed section when present (the "money slide"); newer DocSend guidance: decks with ≥2–3 distinct traction slides perform best; problem slides get under 10 seconds each.

**What V6 does because of it.** Builds in the observed successful order (purpose → problem → solution → why now → market → product → competition → traction → team → model → financials/ask), keeps the ROI thesis legible in the first four slides, gives traction ≥2 distinct slides, makes unit economics the money slide, and places team last. Designed for skimmers, because that is what the data says investors are.

## 6. Simon Sinek — the Golden Circle (V7)

**Sources:** TED: https://www.ted.com/talks/simon_sinek_how_great_leaders_inspire_action ; https://simonsinek.com/videos/ted-talks/ .

**Doctrine.** Communicate why → how → what: start with the belief, then the distinctive approach, and only then the product. People buy the why.

**What V7 does because of it.** The deck opens on the belief ("the night deserves better than instinct" via "we run our own infrastructure"), the how (Rust/Go/ClickHouse/Flutter in-house) precedes the what (product), and traction is framed as proof of the belief, not a substitute for it.

## 7. Jobs-to-be-Done — Christensen et al. (V8)

**Source:** https://hbr.org/2016/09/know-your-customers-jobs-to-be-done (Christensen, Hall, Dillon, Duncan).

**Doctrine.** Customers "hire" products to make progress in a situation. Define the job (functional, emotional, social), show why current workarounds fail, position the product as the better hire, and size the market by the job, not the industry category.

**What V8 does because of it.** The deck is one owner's situation → her job (run a safe, profitable night) → failed workarounds (clipboard, decibel app, hope) → the hire (Clubins) → evidence of progress → market defined by "venues that must run on data," not "club software."

## 8. Andreessen Horowitz — category & wedge (V9)

**Source:** https://a16z.com/series/a16z-pitch-decks/ (principles collection — deliberately not a canonical slide template).

**Doctrine.** Establish the company's category and wedge fast; why the problem is urgent or newly solvable; evidence of product-market fit; defensibility; specific use of capital. Avoid generic top-down "huge market" claims.

**What V9 does because of it.** V9 argues the category (the night's data layer), shows the wedge (sound compliance → operations), grounds urgency in sensor economics meeting regulatory pressure, and sizes the market bottom-up (32,455 venues × price, labeled arithmetic) instead of top-down.

## 9. Tom Tunguz — the 7 investor questions (V10)

**Source:** https://tomtunguz.com/pitch-deck/ .

**Doctrine.** A deck is an answer sheet to the investor's underwriting questions: why this problem now; can this team build it; who uses it and how do they respond; what is each customer worth; what are the unit economics; can the model support large margins; is the market venture-scale (illustrative thresholds: >$100M revenue potential, 50–60% gross, 15–25% net margins — framed as illustrative, not promised).

**What V10 does because of it.** The competitive-wargame angle is carried by an explicit question-by-question structure: each slide answers one underwriting question, closing with a venture-math slide using labeled illustrative thresholds and register placeholders only.
